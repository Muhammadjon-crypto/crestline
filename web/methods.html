Methods and limits

CrestLine cuts three-hour forecast error at Greens Bayou by 46.9% against the standard baseline, and by 54.5% when the water is high. This page shows how that was measured, where the skill comes from, and where it runs out.

Why not “98% accuracy”

Bayou stage is massively autocorrelated. The level three hours from now is usually almost exactly the level right now, so a model that predicts no change at all scores a Nash–Sutcliffe efficiency near 0.9 and an RMSE under 0.25 ft, while containing no information.

Any flood model advertising a flat accuracy percentage is either misunderstanding this or hiding it. The honest benchmark is persistence: assume the level in three hours equals the level now. It is the standard baseline in operational hydrology, and on a river that sits still most days it is hard to beat.

Every number on this site is reported against it.

What was measured

A chronological split: trained on 2018–2024, tested on 60,556 forecasts from 21 September 2024 to 11 October 2026. Those are two years the model never saw. No shuffling, which on a time series would let the model interpolate between adjacent 15-minute readings and produce scores that look spectacular and mean nothing.

Those 60,556 forecasts are not independent of one another. Readings fifteen minutes apart describe the same river in the same state, so the number that actually constrains a claim about flood performance is how many separate events the window contains. It contains 38 independent high-water episodes, counting every run above 5.35 ft and merging runs less than 24 hours apart so one storm is not counted twice. Sixteen peaked above 10 ft, five above 15 ft, and the largest was the 20.70 ft crest of 16 June 2026. The high-water figures on this page are measured across all 38.

Where the skill comes from

The skill does not come from the model being clever. It comes from water taking time to travel.

Cross-correlating each upstream gauge against Ley Road at increasing lags recovers a clear peak for each one:

The lags order themselves by distance upstream: 5.0, 3.0, 2.5, 2.5 hours. Nothing in the analysis knows the geography; it recovered the river's travel time from the water alone.

The lead survives differencing, which rules out shared seasonal drift inflating the correlation: on hourly changes rather than levels, US-75 peaks at r = 0.296 at 4.5 h against r = 0.090 at zero lag. The correlation more than triples when shifted forward.

Where it stops working

That same travel time sets a hard ceiling. Past roughly six hours there is no upstream water left to see, and the model has nothing persistence does not also have.

Skill against persistence by forecast horizon. During high water it collapses from 54.5% at three hours to 7.3% at eighteen, past the time water needs to cross the watershed. This decay is why the product forecasts three hours and not more.

The event that changed the design

An earlier version forecast six hours ahead and looked fine in aggregate: 38.5% high-water skill. Then we plotted the largest event in the test window, 16 June 2026.

Observed water climbed from 4 ft to 19 ft between 18:00 and midnight. The six-hour forecast sat flat at 2.5 ft through the entire rising limb, then jumped almost vertically to 26 ft — overshooting the actual 20.70 ft crest by 5.3 ft. Six hours out, the model had no idea the surge was coming.

For a warning tool, that is the failure that matters most. The whole point is to warn people before the water rises.

Diagnosis showed the crest errors were bimodal rather than biased. Of the six largest events, three were near-perfect (+0.24, +0.33, +0.60 ft) and three were wild over-predictions (+5.33, +4.53, +3.81 ft). Every bad one also peaked early. The model was extrapolating a steep upstream rise straight through instead of damping it.

Adding gridded rainfall cured the overshoot: the same event now crests at 19.8 ft against 20.70 observed, slightly under rather than five feet over. It did not cure the blind spot, and the reason is structural. The model is fed observed rainfall up to issue time, but the storm driving that crest largely fell during the forecast window. No feature engineering recovers information that does not exist yet.

So the headline horizon moved from six hours to three, where skill peaks and the forecast sits inside the travel-time window. Closing the gap properly needs NWS quantitative precipitation forecasts as an input.

Stage IV is radar-derived and bias-corrected against gauges. Gridded rainfall matters here: what drives a bayou is rain over the whole watershed, not rain at one point.

The model is a gradient-boosted regressor (HistGradientBoostingRegressor) over 63 features: upstream levels at seven lags, rates of change over 1/3/6 h, the target's own recent history, and rainfall accumulated over 1, 3, 6, 12, 24, 72 and 168 hours. The 72 h and 168 h windows stand in for soil saturation, since wet ground is what turns rain into flooding and there is no direct soil-moisture feed.

Serving imports its feature builder directly from the training module, so inference cannot silently drift from training.

Built in Python 3.13. The model is trained and served with scikit-learn 1.7.2 and joblib 1.5.2; data handling uses pandas 2.3.3 and NumPy 2.3.5. The live service runs on FastAPI 0.143.0 and Uvicorn 0.54.0, with APScheduler 3.11.3 driving the 15-minute refresh and Requests 2.32.5 fetching from USGS and IEM. The interface, including every chart, is hand-written HTML, CSS and JavaScript with no front-end framework and no charting library.

Limits

  1. One gauge. Trained and validated only on Greens Bayou at Ley Road. The method should generalize to any gauge with upstream neighbours, but that is untested.
  2. No street-level inference. CrestLine predicts a gauge. Whether water reaches a given address depends on elevation, storm drains and local topography, none of which are in the model.
  3. Three hours, not more. Skill is effectively gone by eighteen hours.
  4. No forecast precipitation. Observed rainfall only. This is the largest remaining gap.
  5. Untested at extreme scale. The largest crest in the test window was 20.70 ft; this gauge's record is 33.61 ft. We make no claim about Harvey-scale events.
  6. “High water” is not an official flood stage. The 5.35 ft line is the 95th percentile of this gauge's own eight-year record, labelled as such throughout. Official NWS action/minor/moderate/major stages are not yet incorporated.
