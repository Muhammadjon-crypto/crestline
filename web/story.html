Why we built this

MUHAMMADJON TURSUNBADALOV

I grew up in Abuja, and my twin brother Mustafojon and I were there for twelve years. The rainy season runs about half the year and the roads had no drainage, so when it rained hard the water just stayed where it fell. The rain itself was never steady. Most of the time the water stayed at a level people were used to, and then some days there was far too much of it.

The one time I went to Lagos, the first thing I saw was our van sitting deep in water in the middle of the road with traffic stopped all around it, and what I remember most is that nobody was surprised by any of it. People were walking through the water with their pants rolled up and their things balanced on their heads, and the keke napeps, which are the three-wheeled vehicles everyone uses there for public transport, were packed to capacity, and when one got stuck a few people would get out and push it through for the driver. Nobody was panicking, because they had accepted it a long time ago. There was never going to be anything that warned them in advance, so they just lived with it.

In Abuja we lived with it the same way. We would look outside, and if the water was already up, that decided the day, because cars got stuck in it most of the time and then you were sitting there for hours waiting on a tow with water all around you and no way to leave. The days that caught people out were the ones where the water came up after they had already gone.

We moved to Houston in 2023, and the first time I saw a bayou come up here people didn't seem to know how to act, and it caused a lot of confusion. That surprised me, because Houston has gauges every few miles along Greens Bayou that report every fifteen minutes, which is far more than Abuja ever had. But those gauges still only tell you the water is rising after it has already started.

The bayou we forecast is Greens Bayou at Ley Road, in Texas's 29th district, where Yunus and Alexander go to school. It is one of twenty-three stage gauges in the district, and we picked it because four other gauges sit upstream of it, which is what makes three hours of warning possible at all.

So neither place had the part I actually wanted, which is some amount of time before it happens. Three hours isn't much warning, but it's enough to move a car off the street or take a different way home.
